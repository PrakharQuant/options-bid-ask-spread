Options Bid-Ask Spread

Desk reality vs textbook theory. What actually sets the spread for market makers and informed traders.

Why this matters

Textbooks treat the mid as "the" price and focus on Black-Scholes and the Greeks. On a real desk the bid-ask spread and the continuous shading of quotes around inventory is one of the most important decisions of the day. It is both the market maker's primary compensation and one of the largest explicit costs for traders.

Market maker perspective

Market makers quote around a theoretical value from a volatility surface. Width and asymmetry compensate for three core risks:

1. Inventory risk

  • Gamma risk (highest in near-term ATM)
  • Vega risk (longer-dated or high-vol options)
  • Residual delta, pin risk, book exposures
  • Shade quotes: long lowers the ask; short raises the bid

2. Adverse selection

  • Trading against better-informed flow (news, earnings, superior vol view)
  • Widens when flow looks one-sided or toxic
  • Classic Glosten-Milgrom component

3. Hedging costs

  • Slippage and cost of delta-hedging the underlying
  • Illiquid underlyings produce wider option spreads
  • SPX/SPY/large-cap tighter than single-stock names

Other practical factors

Liquidity volume, open interest, quoted size, balance of flow
Contract moneyness and maturity (ATM usually tightest in dollars)
Vol regime higher realized or implied vol widens spreads
Competition more market makers tighten quotes
Constraints capital, position limits, exchange rules

Many desks manage the spread in volatility space first, then convert to price.

How the industry actually sets it

Real-time, not daily

Modern options market making is electronic. Auto-quoters update thousands of series multiple times per second (often dozens to hundreds of times). Humans set the vol surface, risk limits, and inventory targets; machines do the continuous quoting.

Who decides

Quantitative traders / options market makers oversee the systems. Risk managers set limits. There is no single person "authorizing" each quote. The auto-quoter and risk engine are the operational decision makers, calibrated by the desk.

Proprietary tech

Firms such as Citadel Securities, Optiver, IMC, and Jane Street run custom auto-quoters, real-time risk engines, and co-located low-latency infrastructure. These systems apply inventory skew, toxicity filters, and hedging cost adjustments automatically.

Exchange rules

Exchanges impose quoting obligations (typically 60-90% of time in assigned series with two-sided quotes), maximum quote widths (often $5 in many cases), and minimum size. These are floors and ceilings; competitive pressure usually makes spreads much tighter on liquid names.

When is the spread lowest vs highest?

Tightest (lowest cost)

  • Peak volume windows: roughly 9:30-10:30 ET and late afternoon
  • ATM or near-ATM strikes on liquid underlyings (SPY, QQQ, large-cap)
  • High open interest and balanced two-sided flow
  • Calm volatility regimes with many competing market makers
  • Short-to-medium term expiries that attract active trading

Widest (highest cost)

  • Around events: earnings, FOMC, major news, first minutes after the open
  • Midday lull when volume drops and flow becomes thinner
  • Deep OTM or deep ITM strikes, long-dated LEAPS, illiquid single stocks
  • High-gamma expiration-day near-the-money options
  • Extended or overnight sessions with thinner participation

Rule of thumb: if the proportional spread (ask-bid)/mid is under ~2-3% on a liquid ATM option, conditions are generally favorable. Above 10% the cost often eats edge.

Informed investor perspective

  • Round-trip cost equals the full spread times 100 times contracts.
  • Informed flow itself increases the adverse-selection component, so spreads are wider partly because of informed traders.
  • Prefer liquid underlyings and contracts (high volume + open interest, especially ATM).
  • Use limit orders inside the spread rather than always lifting the offer or hitting the bid.
  • Size carefully to limit market impact and inventory shading.
  • Watch time-of-day and event effects.
  • For multi-leg strategies the net spread across all legs matters more than any single leg.
  • Wide spreads make the mid a noisier estimate of fair value (IV, expected move, skew readings become less reliable).

Simplified mental model

Spread ≈ Inventory risk cost
  + Adverse selection cost
  + Hedging / transaction costs

adjusted continuously for current inventory,
expected flow toxicity, and competition.

Related ideas: inventory models (Amihud-Mendelson, Ho-Stoll, Avellaneda-Stoikov), adverse-selection models (Glosten-Milgrom), and option-specific hedging-cost theory. Empirical work often finds option-return volatility (elasticity times underlying volatility) to be a strong determinant of spreads.
